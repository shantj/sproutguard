How the numbers are calculated

No hidden weighting, no fudge factor. Every figure above comes from four lines of arithmetic:

FigureFormula
Hours per yeardaily hours × 365
Days per yearhours per year ÷ 24
Waking weeks per yearhours per year ÷ 16 ÷ 7 (a 16-hour waking day)
Years of remaining life(reclaimable hours per year × years remaining) ÷ (365 × 16)

Years remaining assumes a life expectancy of 80. The final figure counts only the share you marked as time you'd take back — hours spent on calls, maps, music or work are not being counted against you. And it is measured in waking years, not calendar years, because the eight hours a day you spend asleep were never yours to spend anyway.

That last choice is the one that makes the number land. Two hours a day sounds like a rounding error. Two hours a day of the roughly sixteen you are actually awake is one-eighth of your conscious life, and you would notice immediately if someone took an eighth of your salary.

Why the daily number fools everyone

Screen time is uniquely bad at feeling expensive, for three structural reasons:

1. It is paid in change, never in notes

Nobody sits down for a four-hour scrolling session. It arrives as ninety seconds in a lift, four minutes in a queue, eleven minutes in bed. Each individual withdrawal is genuinely trivial, so there is never a moment where you feel the cost — and the total only exists in a weekly report you have learned to swipe past.

2. The comparison class is wrong

Four hours a day gets mentally filed next to "watched a film". It should be filed next to "took a second job". At four hours a day you are working a 28-hour week, unpaid, for a company whose revenue depends on you not noticing.

3. The alternative is invisible

You never see what the reclaimed time would have become, so it never feels lost. Ten minutes a day is a language in four years. Thirty minutes a day is a marathon. An hour a day is, for most people, roughly the gap between wanting to write something and having written it.

What the number is good for — and what it isn't

An honest caveat, because calculators like this are easy to misuse:

  • Not all screen time is loss. A video call with someone you love and forty minutes on a stranger's argument are both "screen time" and are not remotely the same thing. That is exactly what the second slider is for.
  • Guilt is not a strategy. Seeing a large number produces a burst of motivation that reliably decays within about 48 hours. If nothing structural changes in that window, the number changes nothing.
  • Self-reported hours are usually wrong. People underestimate their own usage by a wide margin. Before trusting your slider position, open Settings → Screen Time on iPhone and use the figure your phone recorded, not the one you remember.

The single useful thing a number like this does is convert an abstract habit into a concrete quantity — and quantities are the only things you can actually take aim at.

What actually moves the number

Intention alone almost never does, and the reason is structural rather than moral. The decision to open the app arrives several hundred times a day. To keep the habit you need to win every single one of those; to lose it you need to lose once. Nobody has that hit rate, and the failure is arithmetic rather than weakness.

What does work is removing the decision entirely:

  • Block at the system level, not the app level. An in-app timer you can dismiss is a suggestion. iOS Screen Time blocks at the OS layer, which is a different category of obstacle.
  • Close the loopholes. Most people block the app and leave the mobile website, the in-app browser, and the "Ignore Limit" button wide open. The six settings that close them matter more than the block itself.
  • Attach it to a streak. Once you have a run of days, the cost of opening the app stops being "ten minutes" and starts being "the streak". That is a far better price tag, and it is the whole reason streak mechanics work.
  • Start with one app, not all of them. Blocking everything on day one fails within a week. Pick the single app your Screen Time report says is the worst offender and beat that one first.

Common questions

How much screen time is too much?
There is no clinical threshold that applies to everyone, because an hour of video calls with family is not the same as an hour of doomscrolling. A more useful test than total hours is whether the time was chosen or drifted into. Check your iPhone's Screen Time breakdown: the categories you cannot remember opening are the ones worth cutting.
What is the average daily screen time?
Widely cited survey estimates put average adult smartphone use somewhere between three and five hours per day, with social and video apps taking the largest share. Self-reported figures run lower than what the phone actually measures, so trust your device's report over your memory.
Does this calculator send my data anywhere?
No. The arithmetic runs in JavaScript in your own browser. There is no server call, no account, no analytics event and no cookie tied to the numbers you type. You can disconnect from the network and the page still works.
How do I actually reduce my screen time?
Remove the decision rather than trying to win it. Block the specific apps at system level, close the loopholes that quietly undo the block, and attach the result to a streak so that relapsing has a visible price.
