How to Stop Doomscrolling

Most advice about this assumes you lack discipline. That's the wrong diagnosis — and it's why the advice doesn't work. Here's what actually helps.

1. Stop treating it as a willpower failure

You don't decide to doomscroll. You unlock the phone to check one thing, your thumb goes somewhere on its own, and twenty minutes later you surface with no memory of choosing any of it.

That's a reflex, not a decision. Reflexes don't respond to resolutions — they respond to changes in the environment that fire them. Every technique below is about the environment, not about trying harder.

The test: if you can't remember opening the app, willpower was never involved. Stop trying to fix a decision you never made.

2. Add friction exactly where the reflex starts

The reflex has a trigger point — usually the home screen, usually within two seconds of unlocking. That's the only place friction matters.

  • Move the offending apps off page one. Bury them in a folder, or delete them and use the browser version.
  • Turn off every badge and push notification for them. A red dot is the trigger.
  • Grayscale mode. It sounds gimmicky; it measurably reduces pull.

None of this stops a determined person. It doesn't need to. It only needs to insert a gap where the decision can happen.

3. Block the entry points, not just the total time

Daily time limits fail in a specific way: you burn the whole allowance in one sitting before lunch, then tap "ignore limit" and carry on. The limit was measuring the wrong thing.

What works better is blocking specific apps during specific windows — work hours, the first hour after waking, after 10pm. A wall that's simply there beats a budget you're allowed to overspend.

4. Give the hand something else to do

The urge is often physical — hands idle, a gap in the day. Removing the phone leaves the gap. Filling it works better than fighting it: a paperback in the bag, a puzzle app you don't hate, a walk without headphones.

5. Use a streak, not a rule

"I'll use my phone less" has no state. There's nothing to break, nothing to protect, no feedback either way.

A streak has state. Day 9 feels different from day 1 — you don't want to be the person who resets it. That's a much stronger motivator than an intention, and it's why streak-based tools outperform pure blockers over months.

Rule of thumb: anything you can quietly abandon without noticing isn't a system. If breaking it doesn't show, it won't hold.

6. Design the first and last hour of the day

Morning and bedtime scrolling do the most damage and are the easiest to fix, because both happen in a predictable place at a predictable time.

  • Charge the phone outside the bedroom. A $5 alarm clock removes the entire problem.
  • If it must be in the room, block the usual suspects from 10pm to 8am.

7. Make relapse cheap to recover from

You will have a bad day. The failure mode isn't the bad day — it's deciding the whole project is dead because of it, and going back to four hours a day for a month.

Build the system so a slip costs you one day, not the habit. Reset the streak and keep going. Trend beats perfection.
